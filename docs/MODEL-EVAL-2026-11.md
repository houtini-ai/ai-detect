# Model evaluation, November 2026

Everything here except the worked example (25 Nov 2026, and its section says so) was run on 24 Nov 2026 on one Windows 11 machine (Python 3.13.2, torch 2.10.0, transformers 5.4.0, onnxruntime 1.23.2), or was read at the source named beside it on that date. Nothing is from memory.

**All runs were on CPU.** Both GPUs were held by another workload (27 GB and 46 GB of 49 GB in use per `nvidia-smi`), so no model was run on CUDA. GPU speed and the CUDA code path are not measured in this document. The CUDA path for `desklib` is unchanged from 1.0.0; for `tabularis` and `conpara` it is the same torch device handling and is untested; the ONNX models use the CPU provider unless a GPU provider is installed.

## What was run

| | |
|---|---|
| Harness | `scripts/eval_models.py` (fetch, generate, run) and `scripts/calibrate_ensemble.py` |
| Raw output | `docs/eval-data/results-2026-11-24-cpu.json` (every margin for every model) |
| Human passages | 25: 12 Wikipedia article introductions, 6 Project Gutenberg books (1813 to 1912), 6 personal essays published 2001 to 2012, and `examples/human-sample.txt` |
| AI passages | 31: 30 generated by one local model (Qwen 27B, served locally) from prompts matching those registers plus first-person blog posts, and `examples/ai-sample.txt` |
| Sentences | 725 of five words or more (241 human, 484 AI) |
| Paired sentences | 24 pairs, the same fact written formally and conversationally (`docs/eval-data/pairs.json`) |

The texts themselves are not in the repo: they are other people's writing, fetched or generated by the script and kept in a git-ignored folder.

### Limits of this corpus, stated first

- It is small. A threshold "at 5% false positives" on 25 human passages rests on one or two passages.
- The AI half comes from **one** generator. Models may do better or worse on text from others.
- Wikipedia and Gutenberg are in GPTTrace's training data (its card says so), which favours those two models on the human half.
- The human text is formal or old: encyclopedia entries, Victorian novels, essays. It has little casual modern web copy. Formal human prose is exactly what detectors over-flag, so the false-positive figures here are probably pessimistic for chatty copy and realistic for careful copy.
- It compares models with each other on this machine. It is not a benchmark. RAID is, and the ranks quoted below are RAID's.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| desklib | 23.9 | 49.8 | 373 | 0.721 | 17% | 0.996 | 97% | 60% | 12% | 23/24 | 17/24 |
| light | 1.2 | 3.1 | 30 | 0.657 | 14% | 0.955 | 77% | 76% | 24% | 23/24 | 15/24 |
| gpttrace-150m | 1.0 | 6.2 | 62 | 0.733 | 29% | 0.969 | 90% | 63% | 8% | 24/24 | 3/24 |
| gpttrace-1b | 2.3 | 88.8 | 875 | 0.840 | 42% | 0.999 | 100% | 54% | 12% | 24/24 | 3/24 |
| tabularis | 1.0 | 13.4 | 117 | 0.713 | 24% | 0.995 | 97% | 63% | 16% | 18/24 | 18/24 |
| conpara | 4.7 | 47.4 | 355 | 0.722 | 17% | 0.999 | 100% | 39% | 20% | 23/24 | 3/24 |

How to read it. AUROC is the chance a random AI item outscores a random human one (0.5 is a coin). "TPR at 5% FPR" is the share of AI items caught when the threshold is set so 5% of human items are flagged. "Over 0.5" is what the model's own raw probability does at the usual cut-off. Load time is with weights already on disk; desklib's 23.9 s was the first torch model loaded in the run and includes importing torch. A passage is about 200 to 300 words.

### The three findings that matter

1. **Every model is good on passages and weak on single sentences.** Passage AUROC is 0.955 to 0.999. Sentence AUROC is 0.66 to 0.84, and at a 5% false-positive rate the best model catches 42% of AI sentences, `desklib` (the default when this was run) catches 17% and `gpttrace-150m` (the default since 25 Nov 2026) catches 29%. Each model's card says the same in its own words: GPTTrace, "Text under ~150 words is unreliable for any detector"; ConPara, under 25 words "the score is meaningless". ai-detect's headline feature is per-sentence scoring, so this is the limit to be honest about.
2. **At the raw 0.5 cut-off every model calls a large share of human sentences AI**: from 39% (conpara) to 76% (light). The 1.0.0 tool labelled sentences at that cut-off. This is why 2.0.0 reports a calibrated figure beside the raw one, and why the web UI colours by the calibrated figure.
3. **The documented contraction result does not reproduce with desklib today.** The 1.0.0 detection patterns write-up (since removed) reported "The software has been significantly improved" at 92.6% and "Software's got a lot better" at 0.03%. On 24 Nov 2026 desklib gives 91.9% and **79.7%**, with the original code at `main` (17609da) as well as with this branch, in float32 and in bfloat16. The formal figure matches; the casual one does not. Tokenisation was checked and is the same through three tokenizer paths. The cause is not established: the February figures may have come from a different library version, a GPU run, or the model that preceded desklib. Across the 24 pairs desklib still ranks the formal sentence higher 23 times, but it puts 17 of the 24 casual sentences over 0.5. **GPTTrace-1B does reproduce the documented pattern** on those two sentences (98.9% and 0.2%), and GPTTrace and ConPara put only 3 of 24 casual sentences over 0.5.

## Model by model

Sources for licence, size and rank: the model card and the Hugging Face API for each repo; RAID rank from every `leaderboard/submissions/*/results.json` in https://github.com/liamdugan/raid at commit 84c2ea4 (16 Nov 2026), ranked on TPR at 5% FPR with all attacks, the leaderboard's default view (https://raid-bench.xyz/leaderboard). 61 submissions carry that score. All read 24 Nov 2026. A RAID rank is for whole documents.

### desklib (the default until 25 Nov 2026)
- **What it is.** desklib/ai-text-detector-v1.01, DeBERTa-v3-large, 434M parameters, 1.74 GB, MIT. https://huggingface.co/desklib/ai-text-detector-v1.01
- **RAID.** 42nd of 61, 0.912. It was first when submitted on 16 Feb 2025.
- **Strengths, measured.** Strong on passages here (AUROC 0.996, 97% at 5% FPR). Loads with stock transformers and torch. Permissive licence.
- **Weaknesses, measured.** One of the two largest downloads (1736 MB; conpara is 1738 MB), with the second-slowest CPU speed. Flags 60% of human sentences and 17 of 24 casual sentences at 0.5. Sentence-level catch rate at 5% FPR is 17%, joint lowest with conpara.
- **Quirks.** Custom mean-pooled head defined in code. bfloat16 on CUDA, float32 on CPU, so scores differ slightly by device.

### light (TMR, int8 ONNX)
- **What it is.** onnx-community/tmr-ai-text-detector-ONNX, RoBERTa-base, 125M parameters, int8 ONNX, 126 MB, MIT. https://huggingface.co/onnx-community/tmr-ai-text-detector-ONNX
- **RAID.** 36th of 61, 0.958, for the fp32 weights at Oxidane/tmr-ai-text-detector. ai-detect runs the int8 export.
- **Strengths, measured.** The fastest (3 ms a sentence on CPU) and the smallest. Needs only onnxruntime.
- **Weaknesses, measured.** The weakest at both levels here (passage AUROC 0.955, 77% at 5% FPR) and the most trigger-happy: 76% of human sentences and 24% of human passages over 0.5.
- **Use.** A quick first pass or a smoke test, not a score to report.

### gpttrace-150m
- **What it is.** nakas/gpttrace-ettin150-text-v5e, Ettin-150M, int8 ONNX, 151 MB, CC BY-SA 4.0. https://huggingface.co/nakas/gpttrace-ettin150-text-v5e
- **RAID.** 4th of 61, 0.998. Its card says the int8 file scores slightly below the PyTorch weights (AUC 0.975 against 0.990 on 300 MAGE texts).
- **Strengths, measured.** 6 ms a sentence on CPU; 151 MB; lowest share of human passages over 0.5 (8%); ranks all 24 pairs the right way and puts only 3 casual sentences over 0.5.
- **Weaknesses, measured.** Passage AUROC here (0.969, 90% at 5% FPR) is below the four larger models, which is the reverse of what its RAID rank suggests. This corpus's AI text comes from a 2026 open model; its card says newer generators are not yet measured. Still 63% of human sentences over 0.5.
- **Quirks.** Input must go through a normaliser that strips invisible characters and Cyrillic/Greek look-alikes (re-implemented in `ai_detect/normalize.py`). Share-alike licence on the weights.

### gpttrace-1b
- **What it is.** nakas/gpttrace-v8s-1b, Ettin-1B. ai-detect runs the repo's 4-bit ONNX export, 951 MB; the full-precision weights are 4.13 GB. CC BY-SA 4.0. https://huggingface.co/nakas/gpttrace-v8s-1b
- **RAID.** 2nd of 61, 0.9993, for the full-precision weights. The card reports the 4-bit export at AUROC 0.9955 against 0.9958 on 300 MAGE texts.
- **Strengths, measured.** Best at both levels here: sentence AUROC 0.840 and 42% at 5% FPR, passage AUROC 0.999 and 100%. Ranks all 24 pairs correctly, 3 casual sentences over 0.5. Reproduces the documented contraction contrast.
- **Weaknesses, measured.** Slowest on CPU: 89 ms a sentence, 0.9 s a passage. 54% of human sentences over 0.5. Its raw scores run hot: the margin 5% of human passages exceed is 7.1, which is a raw probability above 99.9%, so its raw percentage must not be read as a probability. Its card names formal prose by non-native writers and commercial humanizer output as weak spots.
- **Not run.** The 4.13 GB full-precision weights (CPU inference would be several times slower; GPU was not available).

### tabularis
- **What it is.** tabularisai/ai-text-detection, ModernBERT-base, 149.6M parameters, 598 MB, MIT. https://huggingface.co/tabularisai/ai-text-detection
- **RAID.** 21st of 61, 0.990 (entry "Tabularis ModernBERT R1.1 (NFKC)").
- **Strengths, measured.** Passage AUROC 0.995 at 13 ms a sentence. Stock `AutoModelForSequenceClassification`. MIT.
- **Weaknesses, measured.** The worst on the paired sentences: it ranks the formal sentence higher only 18 times in 24 and puts 18 of 24 casual sentences over 0.5. For an editing tool that points at lines, this is the wrong model.
- **Quirks.** Needs transformers 4.48 or later (ModernBERT). NFKC normalisation before scoring, per its card.

### conpara
- **What it is.** SES-Lab-OTH/deberta-conpara, DeBERTa-v3-large with a two-layer head, 1.74 GB checkpoint, MIT. https://huggingface.co/SES-Lab-OTH/deberta-conpara
- **RAID.** 20th of 61, 0.990.
- **Strengths, measured.** Passage AUROC 0.999 and 100% at 5% FPR; the fewest human sentences over 0.5 (39%); 3 casual sentences over 0.5. The same size and licence as desklib with a much higher rank.
- **Weaknesses, measured.** Sentence catch rate at 5% FPR is 17%; 20% of human passages over 0.5. Its card reports false positives of 13% to 67% on academic prose and says the score is not a probability.
- **Quirks.** The most awkward to load. The checkpoint is a pickled state dict; the authors' loader uses `torch.load(weights_only=False)`, which can run code from the file. ai-detect loads it with `weights_only=True` and an allow-list of numpy array types instead (verified: all keys match, scores produced). It needs the `sentencepiece` package and a compatible `protobuf`, and its tokenizer comes from microsoft/deberta-v3-large. On this machine the system protobuf was too old and had to be upgraded inside a virtual environment. The authors' input normaliser is vendored unmodified (MIT).

### Candidates not added
- Linda-RAID (18th): CC BY-NC 4.0, non-commercial only. Not suitable for a tool people use on client work.
- ADAL (32nd), ModernBERT AI Detection (38th): rank below models already included. Not run.
- MELD (6th): gated weights.

What the figures show. The two runs printed the same verdict. Read at its own operating point, no model calls the document AI; `gpttrace-1b`'s raw 89.5% is below its passage threshold (finding 2 above, and the note on its hot raw scores). A raw percentage from one detector is not something to act on.

## The ensemble: method and limits

**Problem.** The models' raw probabilities are on different scales and none is calibrated for sentences (finding 2). Averaging them averages the miscalibration.

**Method chosen.** Each model gets an operating point fitted on the human half of the corpus: `threshold` is the logit margin 5% of human items exceed, `scale` is the robust spread of human margins (IQR / 1.349). A model's calibrated value is `sigmoid((margin - threshold) / scale)`, so 0.5 means "5% of human text scores above this" for every model. The ensemble score is the mean of the calibrated values. Agreement is the number of models over their own threshold. Separate operating points are fitted for sentences and passages. The numbers are in `ai_detect/calibration.json`.

**Evidence.** 200 random splits of the passages into two halves; operating points fitted on one half, methods scored on the other (sentences travel with their passage).

| Method, held-out halves | Sentence AUROC | Sentence TPR at 5% FPR | Passage AUROC | Passage TPR at 5% FPR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Best single model (gpttrace-1b) | 0.838 | 44% | 0.999 | 100% |
| Mean of raw probabilities | 0.834 | 33% | 0.994 | 97% |
| **Mean of calibrated values (chosen)** | 0.831 | 41% | 0.996 | 99% |
| Median of calibrated values | 0.819 | 39% | 0.997 | 99% |
| Vote share | 0.735 | 31% | 0.996 | 99% |

**What this says, plainly.**
- Calibrating before averaging helps where it matters: at the same 5% false-positive rate it catches 41% of AI sentences against 33% for the raw mean.
- **No combination beat the best single model on this corpus.** The ensemble is not an accuracy upgrade. Its use is the cross-check: it shows when models disagree, and it does not depend on one model's blind spot. That fits the common advice to cross-check more than one detector.
- The operating points are fitted on 25 human passages and 241 human sentences of mostly formal text. They are conservative for that kind of writing and unverified for others. Treat a flag as a prompt to read the line.
- A model added later with no fitted operating point falls back to its raw scale and is listed under `uncalibrated_models` in the report.

## Which to pick when

| Situation | Pick | Why (measured above) |
|---|---|---|
| First run, want a result in seconds | `gpttrace-150m` | 151 MB, 6 ms a sentence, 4th on RAID, fewest false positives on passages here |
| Best single score on a document | `gpttrace-1b` | Best at both levels here, 2nd on RAID; allow about a second per 250 words on CPU |
| Must be MIT-licensed weights | `conpara` | 20th on RAID, passage AUROC 0.999 here; accept the sentencepiece dependency |
| MIT and no extra dependency | `desklib` | Works with stock torch and transformers; weaker rank and more false positives on sentences |
| Pointing at individual sentences to edit | `gpttrace-1b`, or `conpara` if MIT is required | Highest sentence AUROC; 3 of 24 casual sentences over 0.5 |
| Checking a result before acting on it | Ensemble: `gpttrace-1b,gpttrace-150m,conpara,desklib` | Shows disagreement; does not raise accuracy |
| Many documents, low-power machine | `gpttrace-150m`, then re-check flagged ones with `gpttrace-1b` | Speed first, then the stronger model |
| Avoid | `tabularis` for sentence-level editing; `light` for anything you will report | Pair ranking 18/24; weakest passage AUROC |

## Recommendation on the default

**Decided 25 Nov 2026: the default is `gpttrace-150m`.** It was `desklib` when this evaluation was run, and the text below is the recommendation as written then.

If the default is to change, the evidence here points to **`gpttrace-150m`**: it is 11 times smaller than desklib (151 MB against 1.74 GB), 8 times faster per sentence on CPU, 4th on RAID against 42nd, has the fewest human passages over 0.5 in this run, and treats casual sentences the way the tool's documentation says the model should. A first-time user gets a result in seconds instead of after a 1.7 GB download. `gpttrace-1b` is the better detector and a sensible "accurate" option beside it.

Three things to weigh before deciding:
1. **Licence.** GPTTrace weights are CC BY-SA 4.0. Running them puts no condition on the user's text or on ai-detect's MIT code, and ai-detect does not redistribute them. Anyone who redistributes or adapts the weights takes on attribution and share-alike. If an all-MIT default matters, `conpara` is the strongest MIT model, at the cost of a 1.74 GB download and the sentencepiece dependency.
2. **Dependency.** The GPTTrace models need onnxruntime, which was the optional `[light]` extra. Making one the default means moving onnxruntime into the core dependencies. (Done 25 Nov 2026.)
3. **Existing numbers.** The 1.0.0 detection patterns write-up (since removed) was written against desklib, and its headline pair does not reproduce today. Whichever default is chosen, its figures need re-measuring before they are quoted again.
